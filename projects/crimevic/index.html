CrimeVic

Where crime, disadvantage and police coverage meet.

An analytical tool for council community safety officers and police planners, showing where crime is disproportionate per resident, not merely large by count, and how that has changed over ten years.

The question

Large by count, or disproportionate per resident?

Raw crime counts mostly measure how many people live somewhere. CrimeVic puts recorded incidents on a per-resident basis and sets them beside two things planners actually act on: disadvantage, and how far people live from a police station.

79council areas (LGAs), plus their suburbs and towns
522neighbourhoods (ABS SA2s) with disadvantage scores
325police station locations and their catchments
10years of data, years ending March 2017–2026
Who it's for

Two audiences, one set of data

Choosing an audience under "View as" changes the opening map layer and the pointers. It never changes the data.

Council community safety officer

Works in a council's community services or social planning team, writes the community safety plan and reports to councillors. Compares crime rates with disadvantage across council areas and neighbourhoods, so the dashboard opens on the neighbourhood disadvantage map.

Victoria Police divisional planner

Works in a division's Tasking and Coordination unit, preparing resourcing and coverage decisions for the divisional commander. Explores station locations, nearest-station areas and residents' distance from a station, so the dashboard opens on the population-served map.

What it does

Every panel answers to the same selection

Pick a council area, suburb, postcode or town once, and the map, rankings, charts and findings all describe that place, for the same year, offence type and measure.

A map with four lenses

Crime by council area, disadvantage by neighbourhood (SEIFA), straight-line access to the nearest station, and residents served by each station's catchment.

An explicit attention rule

Each area's map class, raised one step when two warning signs apply: worsening faster than Victoria, high disadvantage, or stretched station coverage. Written out in full in the glossary.

A neighbour network

Council areas linked to the ones they border, so a hotspot can be judged against the places around it. One click sends an area and its neighbours into a side-by-side comparison of up to ten.

Area profiles and trends

A mind-map profile for any area, ten-year trends against the police region and Victoria, the offence mix, incident locations and the unsolved share over time.

Down to the suburb

Suburb and town counts from the Crime Statistics Agency, with approximate rates (marked ≈) wherever a suburb matches an ABS neighbourhood population.

Accessible by design

Eight data-colour themes, including colour-blind-safe and high-contrast sets, adjustable text size, a 36-step guided tour, keyboard support and shareable links to any view.

Policy alignment

Mapped to the UN Sustainable Development Goals

Proxy indicators built from recorded-crime data, compared against Victoria and, where one exists, the Victorian Government's published target. They show direction and gap, not the official UN series.

SDG 5 · 5.2

Eliminate violence against women and girls: family incidents per 100,000 residents.

SDG 10 · 10.2

Inclusion regardless of economic status: crime in the most disadvantaged areas relative to the rest.

SDG 11 · 11.7

Safe public spaces: incidents in community places per 100,000 residents.

SDG 16 · 16.1, 16.3, 16.6

Reduce violence, and effective institutions: crimes against the person, and the share of incidents reaching a charge.

Data and method

Every number traces to a published table and a rule

  • Map classes by Fisher-Jenks natural breaks, fixed across all ten years so a colour change is a real change.
  • Station catchments as Thiessen (Voronoi) polygons, with residents apportioned from SA2s by area.
  • "Far" neighbourhoods are the furthest quarter of SA2s from a station: a threshold from the data, not a chosen radius.
  • Distances are straight lines from SA2 centres, computed in VicGrid94; not travel or response times.
Honest limits

What the data cannot say

The attention level, far threshold and priority gaps are aids to looking, not official ratings.

  • Recorded, not total, crime. Changes can reflect reporting and policing priorities as much as offending.
  • Rates are per resident. Central Melbourne hosts far more visitors and workers than residents, which inflates its rate.
  • Where, not who. Incidents are counted where they happened, not where people live.
  • Stations are locations. Staffing and hours are not in the data, and incidents are not published by station.
  • Recent outcomes are still maturing. The latest year always shows the highest unsolved share.
  • SEIFA describes areas, not people. A decile says nothing about any individual.
